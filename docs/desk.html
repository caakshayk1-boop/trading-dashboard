    02 / CFO TRACK

    CFO in three years.

    Four questions a day — two technical, two not — plus two field notes. Weighted to retail, the Gulf, and the controller-to-CFO jump. The non-technical ones decide the offer more often than the technical ones do; the field notes are the things nobody asks you and everybody assumes you already know.

    ◆ Technical
    How do you close the books in five days instead of fifteen?Your own 40% reduction · Unilever
    Move work out of the close. Accruals estimated from a model rather than waiting for invoices; reconciliations performed continuously rather than at month end; intercompany agreed on a cut-off calendar with a hard deadline and a default position if unresolved; fixed asset and depreciation runs automated; and reporting built on the ledger rather than rebuilt in Excel. The cultural half matters more than the technical half — a fast close requires accepting a materiality threshold below which you do not adjust.
    A store does AED 12M revenue at 38% gross margin. Rent is AED 1.8M, staff AED 2.1M, other fixed AED 900K. Is it worth keeping?Landmark Group · Alshaya · Inditex
    Gross profit = 12 × 0.38 = AED 4.56M. Fixed costs = 1.8 + 2.1 + 0.9 = AED 4.8M. Store contribution = −AED 240K. It loses money — but do not close it on that number alone. Ask three things: is the rent a committed lease you pay whether or not you trade; what does the store contribute to online fulfilment and brand presence in that catchment; and what is the exit cost. A negative-contribution store with four years left on a non-cancellable lease is often cheaper to run than to close.
    ◆ Everything else
    Tell me about a time you changed your mind after being convinced by someone junior.Amazon · Google · Unilever
    This is a culture question about ego. Pick a real instance, name the person's role, describe the argument they made and why it was better than yours, and say what you did afterwards to make sure they got the credit. The best version includes what you changed structurally so junior challenge became normal rather than exceptional.
    Why are you leaving your current role?Universal
    Never criticise the employer. Frame as pull, not push, and make it structural rather than personal: the scope you want next does not exist there in the timeframe you want it. If there is an obvious reason they will infer — a merger, a new boss, relocation — name it briefly and neutrally before they ask. Anything that sounds like a grievance transfers directly onto how they imagine you will talk about them.
    ◆ From the field
    The offer is decided by the non-technical questions.Why strong CVs lose
    Everyone shortlisted can do the technical work — that is why they were shortlisted. What separates the offer is whether the panel believes you can hold a room, disagree with a CEO without theatre, and be trusted with bad news. Prepare the failure story properly: what you decided, what it cost, what you changed afterwards. A candidate with no failure story reads as someone who has never owned a decision.
    A forecast that is never wrong is a forecast nobody is using.What separates FP&A from reporting
    Track forecast accuracy as a metric in its own right — absolute percentage error by month, by category. Under 5% at group level is good in retail; over 15% and the business has stopped believing you. But an FP&A team that is never wrong is sandbagging, and sandbagging is more expensive than error: it prices in a buffer everyone plans around, and the buffer compounds up the hierarchy.

    The Checklist Manifesto · Atul Gawande · 47/48

    Checklists Are Not for Dumb People. They're for Experts.

    Surgeon Atul Gawande studied why brilliant surgeons and pilots make elementary errors. The answer: not incompetence but complexity. Modern tasks have too many steps for any brain to hold reliably under pressure. His solution, borrowed from aviation: checklists. A two-minute surgical checklist reduced surgical complications by 36% and deaths by 47% in a global study. The lesson for knowledge work: expertise does not eliminate the need for process discipline. The most error-prone moments are not the hard parts of a task — they're the routine parts where experts assume they can proceed from memory. They can't.

    Checklists are not about distrust of expertise. They are about respecting the limits of human memory under pressure.
    Today's actionIdentify your most error-prone recurring process (month-end close, financial review). Write a checklist for it this week. Use it next time.
    The whole book · 12 points
    1. Failure in complex work is usually not ignorance. It is ineptitude — knowing what to do and not doing it.
    2. Volume and complexity of knowledge now exceed what any individual can reliably hold in working memory.
    3. Checklists convert known steps into guaranteed steps.
    4. Two failure types: errors of ignorance (we don't know) and errors of ineptitude (we know but slipped). Checklists kill the second.
    5. Good checklists are short — 5 to 9 items, one page, pause points at natural breaks.
    6. Bad checklists are long, vague, and try to spell out every step. They get ignored.
    7. DO-CONFIRM (act, then verify) versus READ-DO (read each line, then act). Pick deliberately.
    8. Checklists must be tested in the real setting. Desk-designed lists fail in the field.
    9. The largest gain is often communication: forcing names, roles, and concerns to be stated aloud.
    10. A five-item surgical checklist cut deaths 47% and complications 36% across eight hospitals.
    11. Experts resist checklists because they feel insulting. That reaction is the reason they are needed.
    12. Discipline is harder than brilliance, and rarer.
    What actually changes in your head
    • Skill does not protect against the routine slip. Structure does.
    • The resistance to a checklist is ego, and ego is expensive.
    • Writing the list down is what converts intention into reliability.
    • Forcing people to speak up is a safety feature, not politeness.
    Examples
    • Boeing's Model 299 crashed in 1935 because pilots forgot one lock. A one-page checklist let the B-17 fly 1.8 million miles without a similar accident.
    • Johns Hopkins used a five-line central-line checklist and cut infection rates from 11% to zero over a year.
    • Van Halen's brown-M&M clause was a checklist canary: if the M&Ms were unsorted, the rigging had not been read either.
    How to adapt it into your life
    • Pre-post checklist for Instagram: score the caption, check restricted terms, confirm no financial-advice phrasing. Five items, one screen.
    • Pre-deploy checklist: type-check, lint, verify which Vercel project the alias actually points at.
    • Month-end close checklist. Same order every month. Speed comes from the order, not from hurrying.
    • Pre-trade checklist: regime, stop distance versus ATR, position already open? The 59 duplicate NATGAS rows were an ineptitude error, not an ignorance one.
