    02 / CFO TRACK

    CFO in three years

    Four questions a day — two technical, two not — plus two field notes. Weighted to retail, the Gulf, and the controller-to-CFO jump. The non-technical ones decide the offer more often than the technical ones do; the field notes are the things nobody asks you and everybody assumes you already know.

    ◆ Technical
    How do you build a rolling 13-week cash forecast?Every treasury function
    Direct method, receipts and payments, not derived from P&L. Start with confirmed opening bank. Layer in: daily sales collections by tender type with settlement lag; supplier payment runs from the AP ageing; payroll on fixed dates; rent on quarter days; tax and VAT on statutory dates; capex from approved commitments. Roll it weekly and track forecast versus actual variance by line — the discipline is in explaining last week's miss, which is what makes week 13 credible.
    Your forecast missed by 12%. The CEO asks why in the board meeting.Every board, everywhere
    Answer in this order: the number, the driver, the fix, the revised view. 'We missed by AED 14M. Eleven of that is two categories where sell-through ran 20 points below plan; three is timing on a store opening that slipped a quarter. The buying calendar for those categories is now locked four weeks later so we commit on actual trend. Revised full-year is AED 462M.' Never open with context and never let the explanation exceed four sentences before the number.
    ◆ Everything else
    How do you give feedback to someone more senior than you?Any senior finance role
    Ask permission, be specific, and stay on observable behaviour and its effect. 'Can I give you a reaction to how the numbers landed in that meeting? When the forecast changed without the bridge, the room stopped trusting the rest of the pack.' No adjectives about them, one concrete instance, and a suggestion. Then stop talking. Most people either avoid this entirely or overcorrect into confrontation.
    How do you influence people who do not report to you?Landmark Group · Alshaya · Carrefour
    Retail finance is almost entirely this — Buying, Operations and Retail Heads do not report to you. The answer is currency: work out what each function is measured on and frame your ask in their terms. A buyer cares about sell-through and margin, not about your working capital target — so bring them the SKU-level analysis showing which lines tie up cash without turning. Give people the analysis they cannot build themselves, and influence follows. Name a specific instance and the outcome.
    ◆ From the field
    Know your break-even by store, not by chain.The analysis that gets you noticed
    A chain at group-level profitability can be carrying a quarter of its estate below contribution. Compute store contribution — gross profit less rent, staff and directly attributable fixed cost — and rank it. Then, before closing anything: is the lease committed, what does the store contribute to online fulfilment in its catchment, and what is the exit cost. A negative-contribution store with four years left on a non-cancellable lease is usually cheaper to run than to close.
    VAT in the GCC is a cash-flow instrument, not just a compliance task.UAE · KSA · Oman
    Registration thresholds, filing frequency and the timing of input recovery all move working capital. A group filing quarterly rather than monthly holds recoverable input VAT longer than it needs to. Know the filing calendar in every jurisdiction the group trades in, and know which entity holds the recoverable balance — a refund stuck in the wrong entity is cash you own and cannot use.

    05 / BOOK

    The Psychology of Money

    Morgan Housel · chapter 8 of 48. One chapter a day, and where the book has been read properly, the whole thing underneath it — the argument in a dozen points, what changes in your head after reading it, and how it lands on an FP&A desk rather than in general.

    The Psychology of Money · Morgan Housel · 8/48

    Tail Events Drive Everything

    In investing and in life, a small number of events account for the majority of outcomes. Venture capital funds know that 1 investment out of 50 will return the entire fund. Amazon's success is almost entirely explained by AWS — which started as an internal experiment. Housel's lesson: you can be wrong most of the time and still be enormously successful if you're right on the few things that matter most. This means long tail bets matter. Staying in the game long enough to hit the tails matters. Avoiding ruin so you can be there for the rare extraordinary outcomes matters most.

    You only need to be right a few times in your life, as long as you don't catastrophically lose the ability to keep playing.
    Today's actionIdentify the two or three decisions in your career/portfolio that would be truly transformative. Optimise for those, not the daily noise.
    The whole book · 14 points
    1. Doing well with money has little to do with intelligence and everything to do with behaviour.
    2. No one is crazy. Every money decision makes sense given that person's specific history of experience.
    3. Luck and risk are the shadow siblings of every outcome. Neither is measurable, both are decisive.
    4. There is no reason to risk what you have for what you don't need.
    5. Compounding is counterintuitive: $81.5bn of Buffett's $84.5bn arrived after his 65th birthday.
    6. Getting money and keeping money are different skills. The second requires frugality and paranoia.
    7. Tails drive everything. A minority of events account for the majority of outcomes.
    8. The highest dividend money pays is control over your own time.
    9. Wealth is what you don't see. Spending is evidence of spending, not of wealth.
    10. Save without a specific goal. Savings buy optionality against a world you cannot forecast.
    11. Room for error is the single most underrated force. Plan for the plan not going to plan.
    12. Avoid extreme financial commitments — your future self's goals will not match your current ones.
    13. Volatility is the fee for high returns, not a fine for a mistake. Fees you pay willingly; fines you avoid.
    14. Define the game you are playing, and stop taking cues from people playing a different one.
    What actually changes in your head
    • A mediocre strategy held for 30 years beats a brilliant one abandoned in year 3.
    • Most financial mistakes are emotional, so most financial fixes must be behavioural.
    • Survival is the prerequisite for compounding. Everything else is secondary.
    • Your risk tolerance is not a number. It is what you actually do at a 40% drawdown.
    Examples
    • Ronald Read, a petrol-station attendant, left $8m by buying blue chips for decades. Richard Fuscone, Harvard MBA and Merrill executive, went bankrupt in 2008.
    • Bill Gates went to one of the only high schools on earth with a computer in 1968 — one in a million odds. His equally able friend Kent Evans died climbing.
    • Buffett's returns are good but not historically unique; his 80-year runway is. Start at 30 instead of 10 and the fortune largely disappears.
    How to adapt it into your life
    • SIP ₹5–15K/month for the BMW fund. The consistency matters more than the monthly amount.
    • Keep 6 months of KL expenses liquid before scaling any trading capital. Room for error first.
    • Trading capital is separate from family capital. Never one pool.
    • The Dubai target is AED 30K/month, not 'as much as possible'. Define enough or the goalpost moves forever.
