    01 / INTERVIEW

    CFO in three years.

    Four questions a day — two technical, two not. Weighted to retail, the Gulf, and the controller-to-CFO jump. The non-technical ones decide the offer more often than the technical ones do.

    ◆ Technical
    You suspect fraud in a store's cash handling. What is your first move?Any group with an audit committee
    Do not confront and do not alert. Preserve evidence first — secure the system logs, till data, CCTV and reconciliations before anyone knows there is a question. Then escalate per policy: internal audit, legal, and whoever the fraud policy names, usually the audit committee chair for anything material or involving management. Only then investigate. The most common and most costly mistake is a well-meaning manager asking questions locally, which destroys evidence and tips off the person involved.
    How would you build the finance case for entering Saudi Arabia?Alshaya · Landmark · Americana
    Model it as an option, not a certainty. Entry costs: legal structure and Saudisation obligations under Nitaqat, which drive a materially different payroll cost curve; local content and CITC/ZATCA compliance; e-invoicing (FATOORA) from day one. Revenue: a much larger population than the UAE with different mall economics and a younger consumer. Build the base case on 3–5 stores, not the full ambition, with an explicit go/no-go gate after 24 months and a costed exit. The board decision you want is a staged commitment.
    ◆ Everything else
    What would your last manager say is your weakness?Universal
    Name a real one with a boundary. The pattern that works: a genuine weakness, evidence you know its cost, and the specific mechanism you use to contain it. 'I go too deep into detail before surfacing a view — it has cost me time on low-stakes analysis. I now set a decision deadline before starting and present at that point regardless of how complete it feels.' Avoid perfectionism, avoid anything that would disqualify you for the role itself.
    What is the hardest decision you have made in your career?Majid Al Futtaim · GE · Unilever
    Hard means genuinely two-sided, not merely unpleasant. Redundancies, closing a site, overruling someone you respected, or choosing between two people for one role. Structure: the trade-off, the information you had and did not have, how you decided, what it cost, and what you would do differently. Interviewers discount any answer where the right choice was obvious in hindsight — say what made it genuinely close.

    Poor Charlie's Almanack · Charlie Munger · 44/48

    Mental Models: Think in Multiple Frameworks

    Charlie Munger's approach to decision-making: build a 'latticework of mental models' from multiple disciplines. The person who only knows accounting sees every problem through accounting. The person who knows psychology, economics, biology, physics, and history sees the problem in full. Munger's rule: you need 80-90 mental models to think well. The key ones: compound interest (mathematics), evolution (biology), supply and demand (economics), confirmation bias (psychology), systems thinking (engineering). His most important: 'I never allow myself to have an opinion I can't defend with the strongest arguments on the other side.'

    The person with one framework is fragile. The person with many frameworks is antifragile — they see what others miss.
    Today's actionPick one field outside finance to study this month: psychology, history, or biology. Look for one principle that applies directly to your FP&A work.
    The whole book · 14 points
    1. Build a latticework of mental models from many disciplines. One-model thinking distorts every problem.
    2. To the man with a hammer, every problem looks like a nail. Most professionals are that man.
    3. Invert, always invert. Ask how to fail, then avoid that.
    4. Know your circle of competence and stay inside it. The edge is knowing where the edge ends.
    5. Incentives explain most behaviour. Look at the incentive before assuming intent.
    6. The psychology of misjudgement runs about 25 standard biases. Learn them as a checklist.
    7. Opportunity cost is the correct filter for every decision.
    8. Sit on your hands. Great opportunities are rare; act rarely and heavily.
    9. Avoid the three big destroyers: alcohol, leverage, and envy. Envy is the stupidest.
    10. Multiply models: compound interest, scale, backup systems, breakpoints, critical mass.
    11. A great business at a fair price beats a fair business at a great price.
    12. Read constantly. Neither Munger nor Buffett knows a wise person who doesn't read all the time.
    13. Be reliable. Unreliability cancels every virtue you have.
    14. Take a simple idea and take it seriously. That is the whole method.
    What actually changes in your head
    • Avoiding stupidity beats seeking brilliance, and it is far more achievable.
    • Inversion surfaces risks that forward thinking never reaches.
    • Most bad decisions come from one model applied where it doesn't fit.
    • Patience is a strategy, not a personality trait.
    Examples
    • Munger's Costco analysis combines scale economics, incentive design, and psychology at once — no single-discipline read explains it.
    • See's Candies: Berkshire paid up for brand pricing power in 1972 and it returned over $2bn on a $25m outlay.
    • Munger rebuilt from a divorce, no money, and the death of his 9-year-old son to become a billionaire. Reliability compounded.
    How to adapt it into your life
    • Invert the Dubai plan: what guarantees no offer by mid-2026? Then remove those causes.
    • Circle of competence: Indian equities and FP&A. Polymarket stays paper-only until 200 trades say otherwise.
    • Check incentives before trusting any financial content, including your own.
    • Sit on your hands when the regime filter says no. Not trading is a position.

    Chess Tutor · Lesson 18/20

    Thinking Process

    Every move: 1. What did my opponent just do? (threats?) 2. What are my candidate moves? (at least 2-3) 3. Calculate each candidate 2-3 moves deep. 4. Use LPDO check. 5. Make the move. Don't play on impulse. Even 30 seconds of structured thinking eliminates 80% of blunders. This process is the difference between 800 and 1200 Elo.
